
The nearest works cover important subsets:

- CRIB studies direct MTS forecasting with missing values and argues against naive imputation-then-forecasting, but it does not integrate physical graphs or regional coarse graphs.
- CoIFNet jointly models imputation and forecasting with mask-aware time/variable fusion, but it does not construct physical-temporal multi-view graphs or clustered regional graphs.
- MissTSM provides an imputation-free, model-agnostic incomplete time series adapter, but its cross-variate interaction is compressed and not graph/regional-structure oriented.
- BiTGraph models missing-value forecasting with graph convolution and mask-biased propagation, but it does not explicitly combine physical graph, learned temporal graph, and cluster-level graph.
- HD-TTS uses hierarchical spatiotemporal downsampling for graph-based forecasting with missing data, but its focus is sensor-point spatiotemporal systems rather than a unified physical-temporal-regional variable relation framework.
- GraFITi, T-PatchGNN, HyperIMTS, and ChannelTokenFormer address irregular or missing multivariate series with graph/hypergraph/channel-token structures, but not the proposed physical-temporal-regional multi-scale graph formulation.

Therefore, the safe novelty claim is not “no prior work uses graphs for missing MTS forecasting,” which would be false. The more defensible claim is:

> Existing work has studied missing-value forecasting, graph-based MTS forecasting, irregular-time graph modeling, and hierarchical spatiotemporal downsampling separately. However, there is still a gap in forecasting-oriented incomplete MTS models that jointly exploit physical spatial relations, observed-value temporal dependency relations, and clustered regional relations under an explicit missingness-reliability mechanism.

## Abstract

Multivariate time series forecasting under missing observations is challenging because missingness not only removes historical values but also corrupts the fine-grained inter-variable dependencies that many forecasting models rely on. Existing methods either impute missing values before forecasting, learn global variable interactions directly from partially observed inputs, or exploit spatial graphs when physical sensor topology is available. However, high missing rates make both learned temporal correlations and fine-scale graph message passing unreliable, while physical proximity alone may not capture dynamic similarity. We propose **MR-MSGF**, a missingness-reliable multi-scale graph forecasting framework for incomplete multivariate time series. The method jointly models three relational views: a physical graph when spatial information is available, an observed-value temporal dependency graph estimated with missingness-aware reliability, and a regional coarse graph obtained by clustering variables into dynamically or spatially coherent groups. A residual missingness-conditioned fusion module adaptively controls the contribution of regional information, preventing harmful oversmoothing when the coarse assignment is unreliable. The model is trained directly for forecasting, with optional consistency regularization under additional observation masking. We design experiments across random-point, continuous-segment, block spatiotemporal, variable-channel, and mixed missing patterns to verify whether regional graph relations improve high-missing-rate forecasting and under what conditions they fail.

## 1. Introduction

Multivariate time series forecasting (MTSF) is a core problem in traffic, weather, energy, industrial monitoring, healthcare, and environmental sensing. Modern MTSF models often rely on temporal patterns within each variable and dependency patterns across variables. In real systems, however, observations are frequently incomplete due to sensor malfunction, packet loss, asynchronous sampling, maintenance, weather interference, or variable-level outages. In this setting, forecasting must be performed from partially observed historical windows rather than complete input sequences.

Missing observations make MTSF difficult in two coupled ways. First, they directly remove temporal evidence required to infer future dynamics. Second, and more subtly, they distort the inter-variable relationships that multivariate models use for information sharing. A variable that appears weakly correlated with another under high missingness may simply lack enough overlapping observations. Similarly, a graph edge estimated from few common observed points can become noisy, and graph message passing over such edges can propagate unreliable signals.

A common solution is to impute missing historical values and then feed the completed sequence into a forecasting model. Recent studies have questioned this pipeline. CRIB argues that imputation can alter data distribution and corrupt variate correlations when true missing values are unobserved, and proposes direct forecasting from partially observed inputs with information bottleneck and consistency regularization. CoIFNet further shows that mask patterns and timestamps should be part of the forecasting representation rather than discarded after imputation. MissTSM demonstrates that imputation-free, model-agnostic adapters can make incomplete time series usable by downstream models.

Another line of work models variables as graph nodes. Graph-based forecasting methods such as MTGNN learn cross-variable dependencies and combine them with temporal convolution. Missing-value graph forecasting methods such as BiTGraph introduce mask-biased graph propagation to handle incomplete values. Spatiotemporal methods such as HD-TTS show that hierarchical downsampling in time and space can be effective under missing data, especially when contiguous observation blocks are absent. These works suggest that graph relations and multi-scale representations are useful, but they do not fully address a key question:

> When fine-grained variable relations become unreliable under high missingness, can a forecasting model jointly exploit physical spatial relations, observed-value temporal relations, and regional coarse relations in a missingness-aware way?

This question motivates our work. Physical graphs encode true spatial or structural proximity, such as road-network distance between traffic sensors. Temporal dependency graphs encode data-driven dynamic similarity, which may differ from physical proximity. Regional coarse graphs group variables into clusters and provide a lower-frequency relational view that may be more stable under high missingness. However, coarse regional information is not always beneficial. If the cluster assignment mixes variables with different dynamics, a regional branch may oversmooth predictions and degrade performance. Therefore, regional graph modeling must be controlled by missingness reliability rather than blindly fused.

We propose **MR-MSGF**, a missingness-reliable multi-scale graph forecasting framework. It constructs a physical graph, an observed-value temporal graph, and a clustered regional graph. It then performs fine-scale and regional graph propagation and combines their representations using a residual gate conditioned on missingness statistics such as observed ratio, group-level coverage, longest missing run, and edge reliability. The model is trained for future forecasting directly, rather than treating historical imputation as the primary objective.

The expected contributions are:

1. We formulate incomplete MTS forecasting as a missingness-reliable multi-scale relation modeling problem, emphasizing that missingness corrupts not only values but also variable dependency estimates.
2. We propose a dual-source fine graph construction that combines physical spatial relations with observed-value temporal dependency relations while accounting for mask overlap reliability.
3. We introduce a forecasting-oriented regional graph branch that uses variable clustering to provide stable coarse-scale relational information under high missing rates.
4. We design a missingness-conditioned residual fusion mechanism to control regional information and reduce harmful oversmoothing when coarse assignments are unreliable.
5. We provide an experimental protocol covering multiple missing mechanisms and ablations over graph source, clustering strategy, coarse ratio, and fusion design.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Forecasting with Missing Multivariate Time Series

Early practical pipelines often combine imputation with a standard forecasting backbone. This strategy is simple, but it creates a mismatch between the imputation objective and the forecasting objective. The best reconstruction of missing historical values is not necessarily the best representation for future prediction.

CRIB explicitly revisits MTSF with missing values and argues that imputation can distort variate correlations. It instead learns compact and informative representations from partially observed inputs through unified-variate attention, information bottleneck guidance, and consistency regularization. This supports the central position of our work: the model should preserve forecasting-relevant variable relations rather than merely reconstruct missing values.

CoIFNet proposes a collaborative imputation-forecasting framework. It uses observed values, mask matrices, and timestamps as inputs, then applies cross-timestep and cross-variate fusion. CoIFNet supports the idea that missing masks carry useful information and should participate in representation learning.

MissTSM studies model-agnostic and imputation-free modeling for incomplete time series. Its time-feature independent embedding and missing feature-aware attention allow models to process incomplete inputs without explicit imputation. This line is complementary to our work: MissTSM addresses robust token construction, while MR-MSGF focuses on missingness-reliable graph relations and regional variable structures.

Merlin and related robust missing-rate learning methods further indicate that missingness patterns vary across training and deployment. This motivates evaluation beyond a single fixed missing rate.

### 2.2 Graph-Based Multivariate Time Series Forecasting

Graph neural networks are widely used in MTSF because variables often interact through latent or observed dependencies. MTGNN learns graph structures adaptively and uses graph propagation with temporal convolution. ForecastGrapher redefines multivariate forecasting through graph neural networks and highlights the importance of explicitly modeling inter-series relations.

In missing-value settings, BiTGraph proposes a biased temporal convolution graph network. It modifies graph propagation using mask-related information so that observed variables can better compensate for missing variables. This is highly relevant to our setting, but it remains mainly a fine-scale graph propagation approach and does not introduce regional coarse graph relations or a physical-temporal dual graph view.

GraFITi, T-PatchGNN, HyperIMTS, and ChannelTokenFormer provide additional evidence that incomplete or irregular MTS should not always be forced into dense regular matrices. GraFITi represents irregular observations through graphs. T-PatchGNN combines transformable patching with GNNs. HyperIMTS uses hypergraphs to connect observed points through temporal and variable hyperedges. ChannelTokenFormer uses channel tokens and mask-guided attention to handle real-world dependency, asynchrony, and missingness. These methods support graph-structured or token-structured incomplete MTS modeling, but they do not directly study the physical-temporal-regional graph combination proposed here.

### 2.3 Multi-Scale and Hierarchical Relations

Multi-scale modeling is important because time series dependencies can differ across temporal or structural scales. HD-TTS is particularly relevant: it performs graph-based forecasting with missing data through spatiotemporal downsampling and demonstrates that coarse time/space scales can help under contiguous missing blocks. This provides direct evidence that high-missing-rate forecasting benefits from coarser relational views.

P-GUTS uses product graph U-networks with temporal pooling for spatiotemporal imputation. Although its primary task is imputation, its pooled multi-scale design supports the hypothesis that coarse representations can stabilize missing-data modeling.

Learning evolutionary and multi-scale graph structures and hierarchical joint graph learning further show that inter-variable dependencies can be scale-specific and that hierarchical graph decompositions are useful for forecasting. However, these works generally assume complete or relatively reliable observations and do not explicitly model missingness reliability.

Our work differs by treating regional relations as a forecasting-oriented missingness remedy. Regional graph information is not simply added; it is gated by observed coverage and reliability statistics to avoid injecting misleading low-frequency signals.

## 3. Problem Definition

Let:

```text
X = {x_1, ..., x_T} in R^{T x N x C}
M = {m_1, ..., m_T} in {0,1}^{T x N x C}
Y = {x_{T+1}, ..., x_{T+H}} in R^{H x N x C}
```

where `T` is the input length, `H` is the prediction horizon, `N` is the number of variables, and `C` is the feature dimension. `M_{t,n,c}=1` indicates that `X_{t,n,c}` is observed, and `M_{t,n,c}=0` indicates that it is missing.

The model receives:

```text
X_obs = M ⊙ X
M
A_phy optional
```

and predicts:

```text
Y_hat = f_theta(X_obs, M, A_phy)
```

The training objective is forecasting:

```text
min_theta L_forecast(Y_hat, Y)
```

where `Y` is assumed available for supervised forecasting evaluation. Historical missing values are not treated as the primary target.

### 3.1 Missing Patterns

We consider multiple missing mechanisms:

- Random point missing: individual entries are randomly masked.
- Continuous segment missing: contiguous temporal segments are missing for some variables.
- Variable-channel missing: complete historical windows of selected variables are unavailable.
- Block-ST missing: missingness is structured jointly across time and variable or spatial regions.
- Mixed missing: a mixture of point, segment, and channel missingness.

These patterns affect graph reliability differently. Random point missing may preserve enough overlap for pairwise relations, while variable-channel and block missing can destroy whole regions of evidence.

### 3.2 Graph Views

We use three graph views:

1. Physical graph:

```text
A_phy in R^{N x N}
```

It encodes true spatial or structural relations, such as road-network distance. It may be unavailable for generic MTS datasets.

2. Temporal dependency graph:

```text
A_temp in R^{N x N}
```

It is estimated from observed values and adjusted by missingness reliability.

3. Regional graph:

```text
S in R^{N x K},  K < N
A_reg in R^{K x K}
```

`S` maps variables to regional groups. `A_reg` models relations among these groups.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Overview

MR-MSGF consists of five modules:

```text
1. Missing-aware input encoding
2. Reliable physical-temporal fine graph construction
3. Regional graph construction by variable clustering
4. Fine-scale and regional graph propagation
5. Missingness-conditioned residual fusion and forecasting
```

The method is designed around a conservative principle:

> Use regional information as a reliable supplement rather than an unconditional replacement for fine-grained variable relations.

### 4.2 Missing-Aware Input Encoding

We first encode observed values and missing masks:

```text
H_0 = Enc([X_obs, M, E_time])
```

where `E_time` denotes optional timestamp or positional embeddings.

Normalization is computed using observed values only:

```text
mu_n = sum_t M_{t,n} X_{t,n} / (sum_t M_{t,n} + epsilon)
sigma_n = observed_std(X_{:,n}, M_{:,n})
```

This avoids contaminating normalization statistics with artificial zero-fill or mean-fill values.

The encoder may be implemented with a temporal convolution, patch encoder, Transformer block, or MissTSM-style incomplete-token adapter. The proposed graph modules are conceptually independent of a specific backbone.

### 4.3 Reliable Temporal Dependency Graph

A naive temporal correlation graph can be unreliable when two variables share few observed timestamps. We compute an observed-overlap reliability:

```text
rho_ij = sum_t 1[M_{t,i}=1 and M_{t,j}=1] / T
```

Then estimate a similarity score only on overlapping observed positions:

```text
s_ij = corr(X_i, X_j | M_i=1, M_j=1)
```

The temporal graph is:

```text
A_temp_ij = TopK( ReLU(s_ij) * g(rho_ij) )
```

where `g` can be:

```text
g(rho) = sigmoid(a * (rho - tau))
```

or a threshold function. This prevents low-overlap pairs from becoming strong graph edges due to spurious correlations.

### 4.4 Physical-Temporal Fine Graph Fusion

When a physical graph is available, we combine it with the temporal graph:

```text
A_fine = lambda A_phy + (1 - lambda) A_temp
```

`lambda` can be fixed, learned globally, or conditioned on graph reliability. When `A_phy` is unavailable:

```text
A_fine = A_temp
```

This design covers both sensor-network datasets and generic MTS datasets.

### 4.5 Regional Variable Graph Construction

We build a variable-to-region assignment matrix:

```text
S in R^{N x K}
```

Possible assignment strategies:

- Distance clustering: cluster variables using physical distance.
- Temporal clustering: cluster variables using observed-value dynamic similarity.
- Hybrid clustering: combine physical distance and temporal similarity.
- Soft clustering: allow a variable to belong to multiple regions.
- Random clustering: negative control, not a real model choice.

The regional representation and regional adjacency are:

```text
H_reg = S^T H_node
A_reg = Normalize(S^T A_fine S)
```

After regional graph propagation:

```text
Z_reg = GNN_reg(H_reg, A_reg)
Z_reg_to_node = S Z_reg
```

This branch captures low-frequency group-level dependencies that may be more stable when fine-scale node observations are sparse.

### 4.6 Fine-Scale Graph Propagation

The fine-scale branch propagates information over the variable graph:

```text
Z_fine = GNN_fine(H_node, A_fine)
```

`GNN_fine` can be a diffusion convolution, graph attention, mix-hop propagation, or lightweight graph convolution depending on the backbone.

### 4.7 Missingness-Conditioned Residual Fusion

A key design choice is to avoid unconditional fusion of regional features. We use a residual gate:

```text
Z = Z_fine + alpha * Project(Z_reg_to_node)
```

where:

```text
alpha = Gate(phi(M, S, A_fine))
```

`phi` includes missingness and reliability statistics:

- global observed ratio
- node-level observed ratio
- time-level observed ratio
- region-level observed ratio
- longest missing run
- spatial coverage ratio
- mean edge overlap reliability
- region internal consistency

The gate can be scalar, per-variable, per-region, or per-channel. In the first implementation, a scalar or per-region gate is preferable for stability.

The residual gate should be initialized near zero:

```text
alpha ≈ 0 at initialization
```

This makes the model start from the fine-scale graph baseline and learn to use the regional branch only when useful.

### 4.8 Forecasting Head and Objectives

The final representation is decoded into future predictions:

```text
Y_hat = Dec(Z)
```

Primary loss:

```text
L = MAE(Y_hat, Y)
```

Optional consistency regularization:

```text
X_aug, M_aug = additional_mask(X_obs, M)
Y_hat_aug = f_theta(X_aug, M_aug, A_phy)
L_cons = || Y_hat - Y_hat_aug ||
```

Total loss:

```text
L_total = L_forecast + beta L_cons + gamma L_graph
```

`L_graph` may penalize unreliable edges or excessive regional reliance when region internal consistency is low. The first version may omit `L_graph` and rely on explicit reliability weighting plus ablations.

## 5. Experimental Design

### 5.1 Datasets

Primary datasets:

- METR-LA and PEMS-BAY: traffic datasets with physical road-distance graph.
- Weather: medium-dimensional meteorological MTS without a natural road graph.
- ETTh1 or ExchangeRate: low-dimensional generic MTS.

Optional:

- Electricity and Traffic for higher-dimensional stress testing.

### 5.2 Baselines

Two-stage:

- Linear interpolation + DLinear/PatchTST/iTransformer
- SAITS + forecasting backbone

Missing-aware forecasting:

- MissTSM
- CoIFNet
- CRIB

Graph/irregular forecasting:

- BiTGraph
- GraFITi
- T-PatchGNN
- HyperIMTS
- ChannelTokenFormer, if reproducible in the same protocol

Spatiotemporal multi-scale:

- HD-TTS
- P-GUTS-style forecasting or P-GUTS-derived graph module baseline

### 5.3 Main Evaluation Protocol

Missing rates:

```text
30%, 50%, 70%, 90%
```

Missing patterns:

```text
random_point
continuous_segment / Block-T
Block-ST
variable_channel
mixed
```

Metrics:

```text
MAE, MSE/RMSE, parameter count, training time, GPU memory, seed variance
```

### 5.4 Ablations

Graph source:

```text
no graph
physical-only
temporal-only
physical + temporal
```

Regional branch:

```text
fine-only
region-only
fine + region fixed fusion
fine + region residual gate
```

Clustering:

```text
random
distance
temporal
hybrid
soft assignment
```

Coarse ratio:

```text
K/N = 1/2, 1/4, 1/8
```

Gate input:

```text
no gate
branch-summary gate
missingness-statistics gate
missingness + reliability gate
```

### 5.5 Expected Evidence

The paper should only claim success if evidence supports the following:

1. MR-MSGF improves over fine-only graph baselines under high missing rates.
2. Improvements are stronger under continuous segment, Block-ST, or variable-channel missing than under easy random-point missing.
3. Physical and temporal graphs are complementary, or the paper clearly reports when one dominates.
4. Regional graph benefits are not reproduced by random clustering.
5. The residual gate prevents severe degradation on datasets where coarse grouping is poor.
6. The method remains competitive in runtime and memory for low-to-medium dimensional datasets.

## 6. Discussion and Risk Control

The most important risk is overclaiming the value of regional clustering. Existing evidence and local experiments indicate that coarse graph branches can help, but also that a poor assignment can harm performance. Therefore, the paper should not frame regional clustering as universally beneficial.

A defensible statement is:

> Regional relations provide a stable low-frequency relational view under high missingness, but their usefulness depends on region quality and missingness pattern. MR-MSGF treats regional information as a reliability-conditioned residual signal rather than an unconditional replacement for fine-scale dependencies.

If experiments show inconsistent gains, the paper can still be valuable if it clearly identifies when regional relations help, when they fail, and how missingness reliability predicts this behavior.
